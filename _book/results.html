5  Results

Our empirical analysis proceeds in five stages. First, we benchmark how much standard relatedness density and our RF-based Potential explain next-period entry under progressively stricter comparisons (Table Table 5.1). Second, we examine how technology network positions, regional knowledge infrastructure, and accessibility shape diversification potential using region and year fixed effects (Table Table 5.3). Third, we incorporate national entrepreneurial ecosystem characteristics to test whether these dyadic mechanisms persist once national institutions are accounted for (Table Table 5.4). Fourth, we estimate hierarchical random effects models to reflect the nesting of regions within countries and to assess how much variation lies between versus within regional units (Table Table 5.5). Fifth, we aggregate potential to the regional level and estimate spatial panel models to test whether regional potential exhibits spatial dependence consistent with spillovers or correlated unobservables (Tables Table 5.6 and Table 5.7). Together, these analyses test our hypotheses while checking whether conclusions depend on model structure or level of aggregation.

5.1 Entry

In Table 5.1 we benchmark how much standard relatedness density and our RF-based Potential (pred11) explain next-period entry when we progressively tighten comparisons. In the pooled specifications, both measures strongly predict entry: relatedness density is positive and precisely estimated (\(0.055\)), while Potential is larger (\(0.081\)). This is consistent with the interpretation that both capture opportunity, while Potential is closer to feasibility as learned from portfolio configurations. Once we move to the fixed-effects designs and absorb region and year fixed effects, interpretation shifts to within-region changes over time, net of common year shocks. In this setting, relatedness density remains strongly positive (e.g., \(0.089\) in the density-only fixed effects model), and Potential also remains strongly positive (\(0.089\) in the Potential-only fixed effects model), indicating that both track time-varying entry propensity beyond stable regional differences.

The most informative column is the joint fixed effects model, where both predictors enter together. Here, relatedness density drops from \(0.089\) to \(0.028\), while Potential remains large at \(0.086\). This pattern implies that Potential subsumes a substantial share of what density captures, but not all of it: density retains an independent association with entry even after controlling for Potential. In other words, Potential appears to be the dominant predictor in this horse race, while density retains residual explanatory content that may reflect a simpler proximity component that survives beyond the non-linear feasibility signal embedded in Potential. This dominance is also reflected in fit: within-\(R^2\) rises from \(0.014\) in the density-only fixed effects model to \(0.062\) in the Potential-only fixed effects model and remains \(0.062\) in the joint model. This benchmark motivates the subsequent mechanism analysis: rather than treating relatedness as a sufficient summary statistic, we examine how portfolio alignment, network position, and accessibility structure feasibility. This move is consistent with the broader methodological critique that pairwise relatedness can miss higher-order portfolio information; for example, (RelatednessMachineLearning?) argues that shifting from “two-product correlations” to information that captures richer configurations can improve predictive performance. This benchmark motivates our subsequent mechanism analysis: having established that Potential dominates relatedness density in predicting entry, we now decompose Potential itself to examine how portfolio alignment (coherence), network position (betweenness, eigenvector), and accessibility structure feasibility at the dyadic technology-region level

5.2 Network Effects and Coherence Moderation

Table 5.3 presents our core test of how technology network positions and regional knowledge infrastructure shape diversification potential. Column 4, our preferred specification with region and year fixed effects, shows that coherence—the alignment between a technology’s FITS embeddedness and the region’s portfolio—strongly predicts potential (\(0.706\)). This aligns with the capabilities-based interpretation of relatedness in evolutionary economic geography. (TechNetworkResilience?) states that “economic activities are related through sharing various capabilities,” and it links diversification to learning enabled by “cognitive proximity and interactive learning” following Boschma’s proximity framework. In our setting, coherence operationalizes this shared-capability, portfolio-fit mechanism and remains stable across specifications, consistent with its interpretation as a structural feasibility correlate rather than a modeling artifact.

Betweenness centrality, capturing bridge positions that connect domains, exhibits substantial positive effects (\(0.388\)). However, the coherence \(\times\) betweenness interaction is significantly negative (\(-0.284\)), revealing contextual contingency: bridging positions disproportionately benefit regions with lower coherence, offering catch-up opportunities when portfolios are misaligned with these bridge technologies. Conversely, high-coherence regions gain less from bridging positions, suggesting that their feasible opportunities are less dependent on bridge roles and more concentrated in already-aligned portions of the technology space. This contingency is conceptually consistent with a tension highlighted in (TechNetworkResilience?). After reviewing the standard logic that related variety supports diversification, it notes that interdependencies can also transmit shocks and that evidence on how internal relatedness structures shape outcomes is mixed; it concludes that it is “still unclear” how relatedness shapes resilience and that “networks are of great assistance” because economies are “webs” of interdependent knowledge and activities. Our interaction result is consistent with that network-conditional view: the value of a network position depends on portfolio context rather than being uniform.

The eigenvector centrality pattern presents a contrasting story. Technologies occupying authority positions consistently inhibit entry (e.g., \(-0.221\) in Column 4), yet the coherence \(\times\) eigenvector interaction remains non-significant. A conservative interpretation is that authority-like targets are systematically difficult to enter regardless of alignment as captured by coherence. This pattern is compatible with arguments that diversification requires more than proximity. (GoingBeyondRelatednessKETs?) argues that diversification is “going beyond relatedness” and reports that enabling technologies support more exploratory trajectories “only when” they are used by other local technologies, implying a complementarity and integration condition beyond portfolio fit alone. Our null moderation for eigenvector is consistent with the idea that barriers for authority targets reflect complements or coordination not captured by alignment measures.

Distance becomes most informative once interactions are introduced. In Table Table 5.3, the main effect of rawdist is not stable in the fixed effects setting, but the interaction between rawdist and eigenvector is negative and robust (around \(-0.080\) in the distance-interaction specifications). This indicates that authority technologies become particularly difficult when they are also topologically remote from any regional foothold. This mechanism is close to the account in (StrongLinksWeakLinksUnrelatedSurvive?), which describes network links as “pipelines” and states that “the strength of links reflects technological distance,” with the implication that predictable transfer is more feasible through stronger, closer links. In contrast, the betweenness \(\times\) rawdist interaction is small and not robust in the fixed effects specifications (e.g., \(0.067\) and \(0.057\) with large standard errors), suggesting limited evidence in within-region comparisons that bridge positions systematically enable longer jumps purely through remoteness.

5.3 National Context and Robustness

Table 5.4 extends our framework by incorporating national entrepreneurial ecosystem characteristics from the Global Entrepreneurship Index. Column 4 shows that the core dyadic mechanisms persist: coherence remains positive (\(0.709\)), betweenness remains positive (\(0.398\)), and the coherence \(\times\) betweenness interaction remains negative (\(-0.292\)). The eigenvector penalty likewise persists (around \(-0.222\)) without coherence moderation, and the distance–authority interaction remains negative (rawdist \(\times\) eigenvector around \(-0.082\)). This stability supports a cautious conclusion that the coherence and network-position mechanisms are not simply proxies for national institutional variation. At the same time, national ecosystem variables contribute additional explanatory structure. Competition is consistently positive (about \(0.088\) in Column 4), while internationalization is consistently negative (about \(-0.082\)). Other GEI components show heterogeneous patterns across specifications, suggesting they may operate differently in cross-sectional comparisons than in within-country temporal change. This combination of stable dyadic mechanisms and non-negligible national effects is consistent with (RelatednessAndEconComplexityPolicy?), which cautions against treating relatedness metrics as sufficient guides to diversification because institutional and policy context can shape which opportunities are realized. This heterogeneity notwithstanding, the consistency of competition and internationalization effects, alongside the stable core mechanisms, provides sufficient evidence for H3 that national entrepreneurial ecosystems shape regional potential beyond dyadic technology-region characteristics.

5.4 Hierarchical Structure and Spatial Implications

Table 5.5 Table 5.5 employs random effects models that account for regions nested within countries, a specification that matches the hierarchical structure of the data and is appropriate given that the GEI variables are measured at the national level. The high intra-class correlation, \(ICC = 0.60\), implies that \(60%\) of variance in potential is between units rather than within-region over time. The variance components show that country-level heterogeneity (standard deviation approximately \(1.12\)–\(1.17\)) exceeds region-within-country heterogeneity (approximately \(1.02\)–\(1.04\)). These magnitudes do not contradict the fixed effects results; rather, they clarify that while the fixed effects estimates document robust within-region mechanisms, a substantial portion of the feasibility landscape is structured by relatively persistent differences across countries and regions.

Crucially, the core coefficients remain stable in the hierarchical models. In the random effects specification comparable to the fixed effects benchmark, coherence remains positive (around \(0.709\)), betweenness remains positive (around \(0.398\)), and the coherence \(\times\) betweenness interaction remains negative (around \(-0.292\)). Eigenvector centrality remains negative (around \(-0.222\)) without coherence moderation. Distance is more salient in this setting: rawdist becomes significant and negative as a main effect (about \(-0.027\)), consistent with the idea that topological accessibility matters for cross-regional comparisons even if it is less informative for within-region shifts once fixed effects absorb time-invariant accessibility differences. At the same time, the interaction results reinforce the earlier asymmetry. The rawdist \(\times\) eigenvector term remains strongly negative (around \(-0.082\)), indicating that remoteness compounds difficulty specifically for authority targets. The betweenness \(\times\) rawdist interaction becomes positive and significant in the random effects model (e.g., \(0.050\)–\(0.061\)), suggesting that bridging positions may be more relevant for overcoming remoteness in between-place comparisons than in within-region variation. Because these patterns appear most clearly in the cross-sectional component captured by random effects, they should be interpreted as differences in accessibility across places rather than as short-run within-place responses.

These hierarchical results also help justify the spatial models that follow. First, the large \(ICC\) indicates that potential is strongly structured across places, not only through time-varying within-region dynamics. Second, (TechNetworkResilience?) emphasizes that regional economies are “webs” of interdependent activities and argues that “networks are of great assistance,” a framing that makes it plausible that feasibility is not independent across space but may be shaped by neighboring structures. Third, the distinction between within-region fixed effects patterns and between-place random effects patterns suggests that part of the remaining heterogeneity may be geographically patterned, motivating an explicit test for spatial dependence.

5.5 Role of space

Having established stable dyadic mechanisms at the technology–region level (Tables Table 5.3 and Table 5.4) and substantial between-place heterogeneity in the hierarchical models (Table Table 5.5), we test whether regional potential exhibits spatial dependence. We aggregate potential to region-year means and estimate spatial panel models with \(5\)-nearest-neighbor weights matrices. Tables Table 5.6 and Table 5.7 reveal substantial spatial dependence. In the SAR model, the spatial autoregressive parameter is around \(0.376\) in the interaction specification, indicating that regional potential is positively associated with neighboring regions’ outcomes. In the SEM model, the spatial error parameter is around \(0.420\) in the interaction specification, indicating spatial correlation in unobserved components. These estimates are consistent with the interpretation that spatial context matters for regional feasibility landscapes, whether through spillovers or through correlated shocks and unobservables.

The regional aggregate covariates also help interpret the relationship between dyadic feasibility and portfolio-level advantage. Average coherence is positive (about \(0.065\) in the SAR interaction model and \(0.073\) in the SEM interaction model), consistent with the dyadic evidence that alignment is a central feasibility correlate. Average eigenvector centrality of existing portfolios is also positive (about \(0.022\) in SAR and \(0.021\) in SEM), and it interacts positively with average coherence (about \(0.006\) in SAR and \(0.005\) in SEM). This differs in sign from the dyadic eigenvector penalty for candidate technologies (around \(-0.22\)). A conservative reconciliation is that these statistics capture different objects: dyadic eigenvector describes the difficulty of entering an authority-like target technology, whereas average eigenvector of a region’s existing specializations reflects accumulated capabilities embedded in those technologies that may raise overall potential across the opportunity set. This distinction is compatible with the enabling-technology argument in (GoingBeyondRelatednessKETs?), which emphasizes that enabling capacities matter when they are embedded and used within the local system, rather than as isolated targets.

A similar distinction applies to betweenness. At the dyadic level, bridge positions are more valuable for low-coherence regions, reflected in the negative coherence \(\times\) betweenness term (around \(-0.28\)). At the regional level, average betweenness is modest in isolation but becomes positive once interacted with average coherence (about \(0.013\) in SAR and \(0.012\) in SEM). This suggests that advantaged portfolios may combine alignment and bridging among what regions already do, while at the margin bridging targets provide relatively greater benefits when alignment is weak. Overall, the spatial results do not overturn the dyadic mechanisms; instead, they indicate that feasibility is also spatially structured, and that portfolio composition at the regional level can be associated with higher average potential even when entry into specific authority targets remains difficult.
